# Working Outline

Twenty chapters in four movements. Chapter titles, supporting characters, and events are provisional.

The implant works. The plot follows what its success allows him to do, what it makes easier to postpone, and how he begins to take responsibility for preferences he can no longer regard as entirely his own.

Working choices: he is married and works on adaptive infrastructure for Los Angeles. His wife has a professional life and ambitions that predate the treatment. Their marriage contains real affection as well as habits neither has wanted to examine.

## I. The Life That Should Be Enough

### 1. Saturday

Several months sober, he finishes an ordinary week with everything intact. His wife is away for the evening. He eats, starts a film, abandons it, walks, checks his phone. The hours ahead become unbearable precisely because nothing is wrong. He obtains a short-acting intoxicant. The chapter stays close to the narrowing interval between deciding against it and arranging it.

### 2. Again

Anticipation, rupture, return, disappointment, repeat. He discovers that he wants the transition more than the resulting state. Later he drinks because sobriety already seems broken. On Sunday he tries another intoxicant under the guise of an experiment and loses the day. The external consequences are small; the familiar concealment is not. When his wife comes home, he tells her enough to admit a relapse and withholds enough to preserve his explanation of it.

### 3. Doing Well

He returns to work and meetings. At work, he is good at identifying systems that meet their stated targets while failing the people who use them. At a meeting, he cannot find a way to describe his Saturday without sounding ungrateful or exceptional. His wife asks what happened; he gives her a theory. She asks again. A clinician involved in his recovery refers him for an assessment of persistent anhedonia.

### 4. A Quiet Evening

During assessment, the examples trouble him more than the diagnostic label: he can enjoy things, but enjoyment seldom gives him a reason to remain with them. The clinician introduces Adaptive Salience Restoration, an invasive treatment developed for severe, treatment-resistant anhedonia. It amplifies weak endogenous salience; it cannot supply something the nervous system does not produce. He understands its promise before the explanation is finished. They have built a machine for the empty Saturday night.

### 5. What Counts as Better

Further interviews and a review of his treatment history establish whether he qualifies. He and his wife are asked separately what improvement would mean. He wants ordinary life to become sufficient. She wants to stop interpreting every silence as a possible warning. At home, she mentions a professional opportunity outside Los Angeles that they have repeatedly deferred. They agree to revisit it after treatment. He consents to surgery, already imagining the person who will inherit that agreement.

## II. Enough

### 6. Installation

Surgery and recovery are bodily, inconvenient, and anticlimactic. The device is not immediately a finished treatment; sensing, calibration, and gradual adjustment follow. Dependence on his wife embarrasses him, and he makes himself difficult by trying to manage it intellectually. One evening she washes his hair around the healing incisions. For a moment, neither needs to make the procedure mean anything.

### 7. The First Useful Hour

During calibration, he notices that he has folded laundry without repeatedly reaching for another stimulus. Other changes are similarly modest: he listens through a story, waits at a light, finishes a meal without needing the next thing. Some situations remain dull or unpleasant. Clinicians adjust the system against imperfect measurements and his reports. He cannot identify a revelation, only an afternoon he did not need to escape.

### 8. Company

His wife begins telling him things she had stopped bothering to tell him. He takes an interest in a colleague and stays after a meeting to help someone clean up. A person from his recovery group becomes a friend through repeated, ordinary contact. These encounters give him more to attend to than treatment alone could supply. His wife notices his presence and relaxes; he wants her relief to be uncomplicated, and mostly it is.

### 9. Good Work

He becomes unusually patient with maintenance work on a city infrastructure system. He catches a small problem that impatient colleagues have missed, making the benefits of sustained attention concrete. His manager rewards him with responsibility for more of the same work. A proposal he once wanted to pursue remains untouched. When a colleague asks about it, his explanation is reasonable and entirely sincere.

### 10. The Empty Saturday

His wife is away again. He cooks, repairs something minor, and spends part of the evening doing very little. The absence of pressure is unmistakable. He does not need to prove anything by exposing himself to temptation; the evening simply passes. At follow-up, his improvement is substantial. He calls it recovery and feels entitled to the word. When his wife raises her opportunity again, he asks why they would disrupt a life that has finally become good.

## III. Reasons to Leave

### 11. After Treatment

His wife applies for the opportunity. He experiences this as a decision made without him; she reminds him how often they agreed to discuss it later. Their argument reveals that they have been using the same phrase, "when things are better," to describe different futures. He offers practical reasons to stay. She asks whether any version of her wanting to go would be sufficient reason for him.

### 12. Within Tolerance

At work, a resident's complaint draws him into a service problem that aggregate performance measures obscure. He listens carefully and pursues a local fix, but a larger change requires conflict, money, and abandoning work he has come to enjoy. He postpones escalating it. A colleague acts without him. His patience has helped one person and also made him comfortable with an inadequate response; he cannot cleanly assign either outcome to the implant.

### 13. The Person They Prefer

At a meal with friends, someone praises how much easier he is to be around. The remark follows an evening he has actually enjoyed. Later he presses his wife to say whether she prefers him this way. She does, in some respects, and refuses to pretend the earlier years cost her nothing. She also misses an unruly shared pleasure that has quietly disappeared from their life. Neither version of him can serve as the innocent original.

### 14. A Successful Patient

He brings his concerns to the treatment team. His measures have improved, his substance use has remained in remission, and he reports less suffering. The clinician takes his account seriously but cannot calculate how much dissatisfaction his life ought to contain. They discuss adjustments and the limits of attributing changes to stimulation. He realizes he has been asking the clinic to certify both his recovery and his reasons for staying married, employed, and in Los Angeles.

### 15. Comparison

He undertakes a supervised period of reduced stimulation, with support and observation. Restlessness returns unevenly. He feels more urgent, more irritable, and briefly certain that urgency is honesty. An argument with his wife feels clarifying to him and painfully familiar to her. He records what he wants in each condition, hoping comparison will reveal an authoritative self. Both sets of notes contain insight, self-interest, and convincing reasons to distrust the other.

## IV. A Life He Participates In

### 16. Someone to Call

During the adjustment period, an unstructured evening begins to acquire the shape of the relapse. He calls the friend from his recovery group. They get food and sit together; the friend has troubles of their own, and he has to listen. Contact changes the evening without answering the question of treatment. He returns to a clinically agreed setting that offers meaningful relief. Choosing that relief does not settle what he will do with it.

### 17. Her Answer

His wife receives an offer and accepts it. She wants him to come, but she will go even if he stays. He recognizes the temptation to make her decision another test of whether the modified version of him is lovable. Instead, he asks about the work, the place, and the life she imagines. Their conversation includes details he could have learned months earlier. They agree that living apart temporarily is preferable to another indefinite postponement.

### 18. A Reason Is Enough

He takes responsibility for the larger infrastructure problem and brings forward a concrete proposal. Doing so costs him a comfortable assignment and exposes him to criticism he cannot resolve by being clever. He still finds the existing work absorbing. He acts because he has judged the change worth making, without waiting for his job to become intolerable. The outcome is partial: a limited trial, more work, no professional rebirth.

### 19. The Terms

At follow-up, he helps define treatment goals beyond abstinence and reported engagement: whether he follows through on commitments, maintains relationships, and can reconsider how he spends his time. These measures remain imperfect and cannot determine which commitments deserve him. At home, he and his wife make practical arrangements for the move and for visits. He commits to a date for reassessing where he will live. She makes clear that a date will matter only if he keeps it.

### 20. Another Saturday

Alone in the apartment after she leaves, he has treatment, familiar objects, and several hours without obligations. There is sadness, some boredom, and no guarantee that the evening will stay easy. He speaks with his wife about her first days, then spends time with the friend who once sat through his difficult evening and now needs company. End on a small act of attention that requires a choice: staying to hear the rest of something, asking a question, helping finish an ordinary task. The machine remains active. His participation remains necessary.

## Threads to Preserve in Drafting

- Relief must have real value. Give the improved life enough space that losing it would matter.
- His wife should have scenes driven by her own wants, including pleasure and difficulty unrelated to his recovery.
- Treatment changes the conditions of choice without becoming an explanation for every choice. His old habits of postponement and intellectualization survive surgery.
- Keep the device within its stated constraint: it amplifies existing signals, never supplies meaning, dictates beliefs, or makes every environment rewarding.
- Let the work plot expose particular consequences of his decisions. Its resemblance to the implant need not be explained in dialogue.
- The ending is a provisional way of living, not a verdict that treatment, abstinence, love, or unaided consciousness is sufficient by itself.
